SYNTHETIC FINANCIAL RESEARCH NOTE
Title: Interest Rates, Inflation, and Equity Discount Rates
Date: 2026-06-30
Source: Northstar Research, fictional training corpus

Policy setting
The Federal Reserve held its policy range at 4.25 to 4.50 percent at the June meeting. Officials
described inflation progress as uneven: goods disinflation continued, while housing and selected
services categories remained firm. The base case is two quarter-point reductions over the next twelve
months, contingent on softer wage growth and inflation moving closer to the 2 percent target.

The yield curve
The two-year government bond yield ended June at 4.10 percent and the ten-year yield at 4.45 percent.
The positive ten-year minus two-year spread reflected a term premium rather than an expectation of
near-term policy tightening. Investors demanded compensation for fiscal borrowing, uncertain inflation
outcomes, and the supply of longer-dated government bonds. A ten-year yield above 4.7 percent would
raise the discount rate applied to long-duration cash flows even if the policy rate is unchanged.

Growth-equity implications
Technology and other growth sectors usually have longer-duration cash flows. Their price-to-earnings
multiples are more sensitive to a rise in real yields than mature value sectors. A semiconductor
company with accelerating earnings can outperform while yields rise, but the hurdle for positive
earnings surprises becomes higher. A slower easing path can therefore create valuation compression
without an immediate recession.

Capital-expenditure interaction
AI infrastructure spending makes the rate debate important for large cloud providers. These firms
fund data centres, accelerators, networking, and power contracts before the full revenue stream
arrives. When yields are high, investors scrutinise the payback period, depreciation expense, and
free-cash-flow impact of capital expenditure. If monetisation is delayed, the same spending becomes a
valuation and margin risk.
